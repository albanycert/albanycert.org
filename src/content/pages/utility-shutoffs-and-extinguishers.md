Jacob F. led a training on shutting off gas, electricity, and water, and on using a fire
extinguisher, at the September 2026 Block Captain Quarterly Meeting. These are rough notes
from that training — not yet reviewed for accuracy by Jacob or AFD. **Do not treat this as
verified guidance until it's been checked.**

## Turning off gas

- Only turn off the gas if you smell it or suspect a leak. Don't turn it off as a routine
  precaution — utilities generally ask that only they restore gas service once it's shut
  off, which can mean a real wait before your home has gas again.
- The shutoff valve needs only a quarter turn — from parallel (open) to perpendicular
  (closed) — using a wrench.
- You may not smell gas even with a leak; a meter dial spinning fast is a good sign
  something's wrong.
- Move slowly and deliberately near a suspected leak.
- Tie the shutoff tool to the meter so it's there when you need it.
- Automatic shutoff valves exist but can fail — know where the manual shutoff is too.
- Use a non-sparking tool.

## Turning off electricity

- Know where your main breaker panel is, and how to shut off the master breaker.
- Some older homes have fuses instead of breakers — know which kind you have.

## Shutting off water

- If a pipe breaks between your water main and your house (a real risk in an earthquake)
  and your house is flooding, you can shut off water at the street, not just inside the
  house.
- You'll need a water key — sold at hardware stores and online; a large crescent wrench
  can work too, though a proper water key is worth having.

## Using a fire extinguisher

- Your safety comes first: know your way to a safe exit before you try to fight a fire. If
  you can't get out easily, don't stay to fight it.
- A standard extinguisher gives you roughly 15 seconds to a minute of discharge — about
  enough for a small fire (garbage-can size), not more.
- Pull the pin before you squeeze the handle — squeezing first can jam the pin.
- Start about 6 feet back from the fire; closer can blow burning material around.
- Regular ABC-rated extinguishers make a mess (the powder) but the mess is manageable to
  clean up afterward.
- CO2 extinguishers are better for electrical fires but are expensive and not the general
  recommendation for most home fires — including kitchen/grease fires.

- Check/shake your extinguisher every six months to a year so the powder doesn't
  compact.
- Costco has run sales on larger extinguishers in the past — worth checking there.
- One extinguisher per household isn't the ceiling — a neighborhood with extinguishers
  spread across several homes (not just one) is more resilient.

## In an earthquake specifically

- If your gas line breaks or you smell gas after shaking, that's when the shutoff above
  applies — don't shut off gas as a precaution before or without a leak.
- If a fire starts and you have access to a hose, that can help alongside or instead of an
  extinguisher.
- Texting 911 (rather than calling) may get through in Albany when voice calls can't —
  worth knowing this works before you need it.
- Even during a widespread disaster, fires are a high AFD priority — try every
  communication channel you have to reach them, not just one.
